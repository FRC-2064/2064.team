## How we build

The same loop covers a custom XRP ping-pong launcher and a full-size competition robot. A good robot is not the first idea that fits on a whiteboard. It is the idea that still works after research, a prototype, a drive-team test, and a change.

Kickoff is typically the first Saturday in January, when school returns from winter break. *FIRST* reveals the game and publishes the manual at the same time worldwide. Team 2064 starts discussing the game that day. The target is a competition robot that can play in about six weeks.

| Week | Phase | Exit criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Problem and requirements | Musts, wants, and a written problem statement |
| 1–2 | Design and prototyping | One concept selected; geometry proven in wood, cardboard, or PLA |
| 2–3 | Testing and refinement | Drive team has tried it; frame perimeter is chosen; bumper build can start |
| 3–4 | Production | Competition parts cut, assembled, and wired |
| 5 | Integration | Mechanisms on one robot; code and drivers on the real machine |
| 6 | Practice and freeze | Match-ready cycles; spares and pit plan; stop adding untested features |

## Phase 1: The problem and requirements

**Week 1.** The foundation is set before anyone touches a tool.

**1. Define the problem**

* **Identify the need.** What does the game actually reward, in points and in ranking points?
* **Problem statement.** One or two sentences a new member can repeat. "Score fuel quickly into the active hub" is a problem statement. "Build a cool robot" is not.
* **Constraints and criteria.** Constraints are limits: weight, frame perimeter, budget, CNC time, six weeks. Criteria are how you will know the design succeeded.

**2. Research**

* Watch the game animation, then read the manual sections that constrain the mechanism.
* Look up past FRC mechanisms that solved a similar motion. Copy the principle, not last year's illegal robot.
* Ask the drive team and mentors what they need to operate under match pressure.

**3. Specify requirements**

* **Functional requirements.** What it must do. Pick up, store, score, climb.
* **Inspection requirements.** What it must survive to pass. Size, bumpers, wiring, and extension rules are requirements, not polish.

---

## Phase 2: Design and prototyping

**Weeks 1 and 2.** Ideas become geometry.

:::tip
**No bad ideas in brainstorming**
Throw everything at the whiteboard. A wild idea is sometimes the spark for the mechanism that ships. Yes Griffin, even a trebuchet.
:::

**4. Brainstorm**

* Generate more than one way to do the job. A team that only draws the first shooter it remembers has not brainstormed.
* Subteams bring different constraints. Mechanical, electrical, programming, and drive each get a say before a concept is locked.

**5. Evaluate and select**

* Score each idea against the Phase 1 requirements, not against how exciting it looks.
* Feasibility includes cost, days left, and shop reality. A part that needs a week of CNC time in week 5 is not feasible.

**6. Develop and prototype**

* Detailed design happens in Onshape.
* Prove the motion in wood, cardboard, or PLA before cutting competition plate. A prototype that fails on the bench is cheaper than a robot that fails at the event.

---

## Phase 3: Testing and refinement

**Weeks 2 and 3.** Fail on purpose, while a change is still cheap.

**7. Test and evaluate**

* Does it meet the functional requirement, or only look like it might?
* Put the drive team on it. A mechanism mentors can run slowly in the shop is not the same as one a student can run in a noisy match.

**8. Refine**

* Change the CAD and the prototype from what the test showed, not from a new favorite idea.
* Recheck weight, extension, and cycle time after every change.
* Lock the frame perimeter in this window. Bumper construction can start once that perimeter is real. Do not build bumpers for a frame you are still resizing.

---

## Phase 4: Production

**Weeks 3 and 4.** Build the machine you tested, not a new one.

**9. Implement**

* Release manufacturing drawings for the remaining competition parts.
* Assemble and wire the mechanism on the robot. Electrical and programming are on the critical path here, not a step that starts after mechanical is "done."

**10. Document**

* Record decisions, settings, and known failures. Judges, sponsors, and next year's students use that record. This site is part of the same habit.

---

## Phase 5: Integrate, practice, maintain

**Weeks 5 and 6.** A finished mechanism on a bench is not a competition robot.

**11. Integrate**

* All subsystems on one robot, with the battery, radio, and driver station from Bot Basics.
* Run full match cycles: auto, teleop, endgame. Fix the failure that shows up every third cycle before adding a feature.

**12. Monitor and maintain**

* During events, pit crew watches the same failure modes found in testing.
* Spares, a written pit checklist, and a frozen feature list beat a new mechanism invented on Thursday night.

---

## Module deliverable: process check

Show that you can run the loop, not just name the steps.

1. **Problem statement.** Write a one- or two-sentence problem statement for one scoring action in the current game. Include one constraint.
2. **Selection.** Name two concepts that could solve it. Say which one you would prototype first, and why the other one loses on time or shop resources.
3. **Stop rule.** At which phase should the team refuse to cut competition metal, and what evidence would change that?
