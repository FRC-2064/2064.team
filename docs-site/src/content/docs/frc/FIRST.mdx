## What FIRST is

**FIRST** (For Inspiration and Recognition of Science and Technology) is a robotics community built to develop people, not just machines. The FIRST Robotics Competition is the high-school program. Each January it releases a new game. Student teams have about six weeks to design, build, wire, program, and drive a robot, then compete at regional and district events.

FRC is set up like a small engineering firm with a public match schedule. There is a manual, a budget, a deadline, judges, and other firms trying to beat you. Team 2064 is that firm. The name on the robot and the awards submission is the **Panther Project**.

An event is usually three days:

* **Pits and inspection.** The robot has to pass before it plays. Other teams are in the same tent, with the same deadline.
* **Qualification matches.** Alliances are assigned. You play with two partners you did not choose, against three teams you might practice with tomorrow.
* **Playoffs.** Alliances are chosen. Seeding still matters. So does whether other teams trust you.

## Why the season is a game

*FIRST* could hand a team a factory brief. It hands them a game instead. A game has a public field, a clock, a winner, and a manual everyone can read. That is closer to a product launch than a worksheet: limited time, limited weight, a customer (the scoreboard), and rivals working the same problem.

The current game is **REBUILT presented by Haas**. The theme is industrial on purpose. Haas is a machine-tool company. The field asks robots to move fuel, cross a built environment, and climb a structure before time runs out. That maps to real work students can name:

* **Manufacturing and throughput.** Fuel comes back onto the field. The job is cycle time, uptime, and not jamming, which is how a production line is judged.
* **Energy and logistics.** Getting material from a depot or the floor into a target, on a schedule, is a warehouse and plant problem.
* **Construction and access.** The tower climb is a height, load, and safety problem, not a video-game boss.
* **A deadline.** Six weeks to a robot that has to pass inspection is a bid schedule.

The game is the wrapper so a school team can practice that job in public, with awards and a match schedule, instead of only reading about it. When you answer the mission question, connect the theme to one of those industries, then say why a match clock teaches the constraint better than an open-ended project would.

## Gracious Professionalism

Gracious Professionalism means competing at full effort and still treating people well. It is not "be nice instead of trying." It is not "let them win." Woodie Flowers' standard, and the one this team uses, is high play and high conduct at the same time.

On 2064 that looks like this:

* Play the match to win. Do not sandbag a semifinal to protect a feeling.
* No trash talk about another team's robot, drivers, or school. Critique a mechanism. Do not critique the students who built it.
* In our shop, disagree about a design and then do the job the lead assigned. The argument ends when the decision is made.
* Leave the pit cleaner than you found it. Return a borrowed tool the same day.
* Lose a match and shake hands. Win a match and shake hands.

A professional shop is part of the same rule. Safety clearance, a battery that is not left on the floor, and a mentor call before you enable the robot are how the Panther Project looks like a team another school would trust. A win with a messy pit and a borrowed tool still out fails this standard.

## Coopertition

Coopertition means we can share and still play to win. Helping a rival does not mean handing them the match. It means the field works, more robots pass inspection, and alliances have someone worth picking.

A scenario you can use on the form: it is qualification day, our robot has already passed inspection, and the team next to us cannot get a battery connector to seat before their next match. Loaning a cable or a tool, and staying while they ask a question we actually know, gets their robot on the field. We still play them at full strength if we meet in a match. We do not give them our match plan, and we do not leave our own down robot to do their repair.

Help that fits:

* Loan a tool, a bolt, or a battery cable so their robot makes the next match.
* Answer a rules question you actually know. If you do not know, say so and point them at a referee or their manual.
* Share a scouting note about a common field problem. Do not share alliance strategy for the match you are about to play against them.
* After 2064 is eliminated, help a team still in the tournament get back on the field.

Help that does not fit:

* Doing their inspection fixes while our robot is down.
* Giving away a mechanism design the team has not agreed to share.
* Softening our defense because we like them.

The long-term return is not a trophy for kindness. Teams remember who loaned the cable. Alliance captains pick teams they trust. Awards interviews notice a pit other schools will walk into. The next event is easier if 2064 can ask for help because we already gave it. That is the benefit to the Panther Project.

## Roles that are not the robot

A robot that scores and a team that cannot explain, scout, or fund itself is a one-event team. These roles are part of FRC culture. The form asks for two. Any two of these count if you say why.

| Role | Why the team cannot drop it |
| --- | --- |
| Scouting and strategy | Qualification matches are assigned partners. Someone has to know who can climb, who can cycle fuel, and which ranking point is in reach. |
| Business and sponsors | The robot has a budget. Sponsors and the school pay for batteries, field trips, and the event. That pitch is a student job. |
| Imagery and awards | Judges do not watch every match. Photos, a team story, and a pit that looks intentional are how the Panther Project is judged off the field. |
| Outreach | *FIRST* measures community impact, not only ranking. Demos and school visits are the public half of the mission. |

Mechanical, electrical, programming, and drive still build and run the robot. A new member is placed on one area. You can ask to try another after you have been useful on the first. Mentors and subteam leads assign the work. If you have no job, ask a lead. Do not invent one that uses a tool you are not signed off on.
